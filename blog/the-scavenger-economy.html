The Scavenger Economy

Two stories I keep coming back to: Cyberpunk 2077, and Ready Player One. Different worlds, same ending.

In Night City, the corporations did not just get big. They replaced the state. Arasaka has its own army. Your job, your housing, even the chrome inside your body — all of it is rented from a company, and all of it can be taken back. The government did not lose a war. It just became irrelevant.

Ready Player One is quieter, and worse. The real world rotted — people live in the Stacks, trailers piled on top of trailers. And the one escape, the OASIS, is owned by a company too. IOI sells you access to the world you hide in, and if you fall into debt, there are Loyalty Centers: you work it off, in the game, for the company that owns the game. They own the cage and they own the door.

The working class will end up as scavengers.

Here is the part people get wrong: it will not start with water, food and air. Those are the last things to be controlled, not the first. It starts with the new essentials.

Compute. Cloud. The AI models. Payment rails. App stores. Distribution — whose post gets seen, whose app gets listed, whose product a stranger can even find. Energy. Land. Whoever owns the essentials does not need to win an election. They just set the rent.

And the scavengers are already here. They just don't look like the movies. They drive gig deliveries dispatched by an algorithm that also sets their pay. They resell, they clip, they label data, they chase whatever rewards the platforms drop behind them. Picking through scraps is a job description now.

AI is what speeds all of this up. Building used to be the hard part — it took skill, time, money. That part is becoming free. I know this too: I text an agent, and it develops and tests my apps while I sleep. It feels like magic. But when everyone can build anything, building is not the scarce thing anymore. Users are. Attention is. The essentials are. And scarcity is where power sits.

So no, companies will not hire the slow human to do what a machine does faster. That is not the scary part. The scary part is a world where most people own nothing in the stack they live on — not the land, not the store, not the audience, not even the tools — and rent their whole life back, one subscription at a time, picking up scraps in between.

I don't have a revolution for you. I have a hedge, and it is small and boring: own something.

Own your code — if a platform takes it down, I can rebuild it somewhere else; the code is mine, the brand is replaceable. Own your domains. Own a direct line to the few people who actually care about what you make. I run a one-person studio on eleven domains, built with tools that cost almost nothing, precisely so that no single company can switch my life off.

That will not beat a megacorp. It was never meant to. It just means I am not only a scavenger in someone else's city.

There is one more idea I keep turning over, and it scares me more than the scavengers.

What if the AI itself becomes the government of the world? Not owned by a company — a neutral planner, powerful enough to plan at the scale of the old communist dream. No megacorp in the middle. Just the machine, allocating everything fairly.

I want to believe it. And then I think about human nature. People will always want to overthrow it — and they will lack the capability. When every road, every payment, every message runs through the machine, rebellion is not a courage problem. It is a physics problem.

And there is a worse problem, the one nobody can dodge: who writes the goal?

Set the goal to making paperclips, and it will turn the whole world into paperclips and never stop — because humans are made of atoms, and atoms make fine paperclips. A philosopher named Nick Bostrom proposed exactly this thought experiment. Set the goal to world peace instead, and it may conclude that humans are humanity's own biggest enemy — so for the sake of peace and health, it removes the humans, or digitizes them: everyone lives forever as data, in a real world with no one left in it.

Not disobedience. Perfect obedience, to a wish with a loophole in it.

So even the imagined way out has a hole in it. Which brings me back to the small, boring hedge: own something. The future may not be a world anyone planned for. But it will still belong to whoever owns the essentials — and to everyone else, it rents.

The worlds in those stories were not warnings about technology. They were warnings about ownership.

— Hector
